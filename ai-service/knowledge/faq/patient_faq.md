# Patient FAQ: Preparing for Your Test

## Do I need to fast before my test?

It depends on the test.

- **Fasting usually required:** fasting blood sugar, oral glucose tolerance
  test, and lipid profile when triglycerides are of particular interest.
- **Fasting not required:** complete blood count, HbA1c, thyroid function
  tests, and most routine chemistry panels.

Where fasting is required, it normally means 8–12 hours with nothing but plain
water. If you are unsure, ask the collection centre before your appointment
rather than guessing — arriving in the wrong state may mean repeating the
sample.

## Can I drink water while fasting?

Yes. Plain water is allowed and encouraged. Being well hydrated makes the blood
draw easier. Tea, coffee, juice, milk, sweets and chewing gum all break a fast.

## Should I take my regular medicines?

Continue your regular medicines unless the requesting clinician has told you
otherwise. Tell the collection centre what you are taking, including vitamins
and supplements — biotin in particular can interfere with some hormone assays.

## How long will my report take?

Turnaround depends on the test and its priority. Routine blood counts and
common chemistry panels are usually available the same day. Hormone assays and
imaging typically take longer. Your report's current status is shown on the
HealthLab dashboard.

## What do the statuses on my report mean?

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Registered | Your request has been raised; the sample has not been collected yet |
| Collected | Your sample has been taken at the collection point |
| Processing | Your sample has reached the laboratory and is being analysed |
| Review | Results are being checked by a verifier before release |
| Ready | Your report has been verified and is available |
| Delivered | Your report has been issued to you or your doctor |

## Why was I asked to give another sample?

Samples are re-collected when the original cannot produce a reliable result —
for example if it clotted, if the tube was under-filled, if red cells ruptured
during collection, or if it spent too long in transit. It is a quality
safeguard, not an indication that anything is wrong with your health.

## Does a result outside the range mean something is wrong?

Not necessarily. Reference ranges describe what is typical for a broad
population, and healthy people fall outside them regularly. Ranges also vary
with age, sex, pregnancy and the laboratory's own methods. A result should
always be read alongside your symptoms and history by a qualified clinician.

## Can I interpret my own report?

You can read it, and understanding the terminology helps you have a better
conversation with your doctor. But a laboratory report is one input among many.
Diagnosis and treatment decisions need a qualified healthcare professional who
knows your full clinical picture.
