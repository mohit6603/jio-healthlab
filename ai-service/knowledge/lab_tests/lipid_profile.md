# Lipid Profile (Cholesterol Panel)

## What a lipid profile measures

A lipid profile measures the fats circulating in blood. Cholesterol is not
transported freely — it travels packaged inside lipoprotein particles, and the
panel reports several of these separately because they behave very differently.

## Components of the panel

- **Total cholesterol** — the overall amount of cholesterol across all
  particles.
- **LDL cholesterol** — low-density lipoprotein. LDL particles deliver
  cholesterol to tissues, and higher levels are associated with greater
  cardiovascular risk. Often labelled "bad" cholesterol as shorthand.
- **HDL cholesterol** — high-density lipoprotein. HDL particles carry
  cholesterol back to the liver for disposal, and higher levels are generally
  associated with lower risk. Often labelled "good" cholesterol.
- **Triglycerides** — the main storage form of dietary fat, strongly influenced
  by recent meals and alcohol.
- **Non-HDL cholesterol** — total cholesterol minus HDL, a single number that
  captures all the potentially atherogenic particles.
- **Ratios** — such as total cholesterol to HDL, used in some risk calculators.

## How LDL is obtained

Many laboratories *calculate* LDL from the other measured values rather than
measuring it directly. Calculated LDL becomes unreliable when triglycerides are
markedly raised, in which case a direct LDL measurement or a repeat fasting
sample may be requested.

## Explaining a lipid profile in simple terms

Think of cholesterol as cargo and lipoproteins as the vehicles that carry it.
LDL vehicles drop cargo off in the walls of blood vessels, where it can
accumulate. HDL vehicles pick cargo back up and return it to the liver.
Triglycerides are a separate fuel store that rises sharply after eating. A
lipid profile counts the cargo and describes which vehicles are carrying it.

## Why a clinician orders a lipid profile

Reasons include cardiovascular risk assessment, family history of early heart
disease, monitoring lipid-lowering treatment, and follow-up of a previously
abnormal result.

## Preparation

Fasting requirements have relaxed in many settings, but a 9–12 hour fast is
still commonly requested when triglycerides are of particular interest, because
they rise substantially after a meal. Alcohol in the preceding 24 hours can
raise triglycerides considerably. Where a fasting sample is requested, the
patient may drink water.

## Sample requirements

The sample is serum or plasma. If the sample is visibly lipaemic — cloudy with
fat — the laboratory may flag the result or request a fasting repeat, because
lipaemia interferes with several measurements.
