# Blood Glucose Testing

## What blood glucose tests measure

Blood glucose tests measure the concentration of sugar (glucose) circulating in
the blood. Glucose is the body's primary immediate energy source, and its level
is regulated largely by insulin. Different glucose tests capture different time
windows.

## The main glucose tests

### Fasting blood sugar (FBS)

Measures glucose after the patient has taken nothing but water for 8–12 hours.
It reflects baseline glucose regulation when no recent meal is influencing the
result.

### Random blood sugar (RBS)

Measures glucose at any time of day without regard to meals. It is convenient
for screening but harder to interpret in isolation.

### Postprandial blood sugar (PPBS)

Measures glucose two hours after a meal, showing how effectively the body
clears a glucose load.

### Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)

The patient fasts, a baseline sample is taken, a standard glucose drink is
given, and further samples are drawn at fixed intervals. It characterises the
glucose response over time and is used in pregnancy screening among other
indications.

## What HbA1c measures

**HbA1c** (glycated haemoglobin) measures the proportion of haemoglobin that
has glucose chemically attached to it. Because red blood cells live roughly
three months, HbA1c reflects **average** blood glucose over approximately the
preceding 8–12 weeks rather than at a single moment.

The purpose of an HbA1c test is therefore twofold:

1. To assess longer-term glucose control without requiring fasting.
2. To monitor how well an existing treatment plan is working over time.

HbA1c is reported as a percentage, or in mmol/mol. It can be misleading in
conditions that change red cell lifespan, such as certain anaemias or recent
significant blood loss.

## Preparation

- Fasting glucose and OGTT **require** a fasting period — normally 8–12 hours,
  water permitted.
- HbA1c and random glucose do **not** require fasting.
- The laboratory should be told about the patient's fasting status, because it
  changes how the result is reported.

## Sample requirements

Glucose is usually collected into a fluoride-oxalate tube (commonly grey-
topped), which slows the breakdown of glucose by blood cells after collection.
A delay in separating an ordinary tube can lower the measured glucose and
produce a falsely low result. HbA1c is collected into an EDTA tube.

## Understanding the terminology

- **Hyperglycaemia** — glucose above the expected range.
- **Hypoglycaemia** — glucose below the expected range.
- **Impaired fasting glucose** — a fasting result above the normal range but
  below the diabetic threshold.
- **Glycation** — the non-enzymatic attachment of glucose to a protein, the
  process HbA1c measures.
