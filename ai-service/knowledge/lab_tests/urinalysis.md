# Urinalysis (Urine Test)

## What a urinalysis measures

A urinalysis examines a urine sample in up to three ways. Together they screen
for conditions affecting the kidneys and urinary tract, and can reveal
metabolic changes that a blood test might also show.

- **Physical** — colour, clarity and concentration.
- **Chemical** — a reagent strip reads several analytes at once.
- **Microscopic** — the sediment is examined under a microscope when the
  physical or chemical findings warrant it.

## The reagent strip panel

| Pad | What it indicates |
|---|---|
| Specific gravity | How concentrated or dilute the urine is |
| pH | Acidity, influenced by diet and by some conditions |
| Protein | Protein that the kidneys would normally retain |
| Glucose | Sugar appearing in urine when blood levels exceed the renal threshold |
| Ketones | Products of fat metabolism |
| Blood | Red cells or haemoglobin present |
| Leukocyte esterase | An enzyme released by white blood cells |
| Nitrite | Produced by some bacteria that reduce dietary nitrate |
| Bilirubin and urobilinogen | Pigments processed by the liver |

## Microscopic examination

The sample is centrifuged and the sediment examined for red cells, white
cells, epithelial cells, casts and crystals. **Casts** are cylindrical
structures formed in the kidney tubules; their presence localises a finding to
the kidney rather than further down the tract.

## Why a clinician orders a urinalysis

Reasons include investigating urinary symptoms, screening in pregnancy,
monitoring people with diabetes or high blood pressure, pre-operative
assessment, and follow-up of a previously abnormal result.

## Understanding the terminology

- **Proteinuria** — protein present in urine.
- **Haematuria** — blood present in urine, which may be visible or detected
  only on testing.
- **Pyuria** — white cells present in urine.
- **Specific gravity** — a measure of urine concentration relative to water.

## Sample requirements

A **midstream clean-catch** sample is standard: the first part of the stream is
discarded so that cells and bacteria from the urethral opening do not
contaminate the sample. A sterile container is used.

Collection instructions matter more here than for most tests, because a poorly
collected sample produces findings that reflect contamination rather than the
patient.

## Timing and transport

A first-morning sample is the most concentrated and is preferred for screening.
Urine should reach the laboratory within two hours at room temperature, or be
refrigerated. A delayed sample changes: bacteria multiply, pH rises and casts
break down, so a late sample can read very differently from the same urine
tested promptly.

## Turnaround

Physical and chemical testing is quick and is normally resulted within a few
hours. Microscopy adds time. Urgent samples are prioritised.
