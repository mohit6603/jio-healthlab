# Kidney Function Tests

## What a kidney function test measures

A kidney function test — often called a renal profile or renal function test —
measures substances in blood that the kidneys are responsible for filtering and
regulating. Together they indicate how effectively the kidneys are clearing
waste and maintaining fluid and electrolyte balance.

## Components of the panel

- **Creatinine** — a waste product of normal muscle metabolism, cleared almost
  entirely by the kidneys. Because production is relatively steady for a given
  person, blood creatinine is a practical marker of filtration.
- **Urea (BUN)** — a waste product of protein breakdown. It is influenced by
  hydration, diet and bleeding in the gut as well as by kidney function.
- **eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate)** — a calculated estimate of
  how much blood the kidneys filter per minute, derived from creatinine along
  with age and sex.
- **Electrolytes** — sodium, potassium, chloride and bicarbonate, which the
  kidneys regulate closely.
- **Uric acid** — another nitrogen waste product, sometimes included.
- **Albumin and total protein** — proteins that should largely be retained
  rather than lost in urine.

## Why eGFR is an estimate

eGFR is calculated, not measured directly. The equations assume a typical
relationship between creatinine and muscle mass, so the estimate is less
reliable at extremes of body composition, in pregnancy, and in people with
very high or very low muscle mass. It is reported as a range-based estimate for
this reason.

## Why a clinician orders a renal profile

Reasons include routine health screening, monitoring people with diabetes or
high blood pressure, checking kidney function before and during treatment with
drugs cleared by the kidney, investigating swelling or changes in urine output,
and assessing dehydration.

## Understanding the terminology

- **Glomerulus** — the filtering unit of the kidney.
- **Azotaemia** — a build-up of nitrogen waste products in the blood.
- **Proteinuria** — protein present in urine, which normally retains it.
- **Electrolyte imbalance** — sodium, potassium or bicarbonate outside the
  expected range.

## Preparation and sample requirements

Fasting is not usually required, though it may be requested when the renal
profile is bundled with a lipid or glucose panel. Heavy exercise and large
protein meals shortly before collection can raise creatinine and urea slightly.

Samples are serum or lithium-heparin plasma. Potassium is particularly
sensitive to handling: a difficult draw, vigorous mixing or delayed separation
can release potassium from cells and produce a falsely raised result, which is
why the laboratory may request a repeat sample.
